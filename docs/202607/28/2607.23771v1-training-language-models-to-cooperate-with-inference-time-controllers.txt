## Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) performance in-creasingly depends not only on the base model, but also on the inference-time controller used to organize reasoning. Existing post-training methods, however, typically optimize for a sin-gle fixed interaction pattern, despite real de-ployments relying on diverse controllers such as Chain-of-Thought, self-consistency, debate, planning, and verification pipelines. This cre-ates a training–deployment mismatch and lim-its transfer to new workflows. We introduce 

CALM (Controller-Aware Language Models), a post-training framework that explicitly places controllers in the training loop. We formulate controller-aware post-training as multi-task rein-forcement learning over controller-induced inter-action protocols, where controllers are composi-tions of reusable local reasoning modules. This structure also induces a module-level decompo-sition of mixed-controller training under a turn-level GRPO objective, enabling a systematic study of controller and module-aware training strategies. We evaluate CALM on held-out controller compo-sitions and broader controller shifts, showing that controller-aware post-training improves general-ization across inference-time workflows beyond single-controller optimization.

Artificial intelligence systems based on large language mod-els (LLMs) are rapidly evolving from standalone predictors into general-purpose agents capable of reasoning, planning, and interacting with complex environments (Brown et al., 2020; Chowdhery et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023). This shift has made the performance of LLM agents depend not only on the capability of the underlying model, but also on how inference is organized and orchestrated at test time. A growing family of inference-time procedures has emerged to enhance LLM reasoning and agency. Foundational meth-ods include Chain-of-Thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022), Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023), Tree-of-thought, and Algorithm-of-thought (Yao et al., 2023; Sel et al., 2024), while more structured pipelines integrate Self-refinement (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), Multi-agent de-bate (Du et al., 2023), Tool-augmented reasoning (Schick et al., 2023), and workflows that combine planning, mem-ory, and external actions (Yao et al., 2022). We refer to such inference-time procedures as controllers : a controller specifies how the model is queried over one or more calls, in-cluding the number of turns, the active role at each turn, and any aggregation, verification, or search logic. As controllers are now becoming sophisticated enough to be automatically searched and optimized (Hu et al., 2025; Saad-Falcon et al., 2024), the performance still depends on the base model which is typically post-trained for a single fixed interac-tion pattern. For example, prior work discusses fine-tuning strategies for specific inference-time techniques such as Best-of-N sampling (Chow et al., 2025). This mismatch is consequential: modern deployments rely on a family of con-trollers that often share recurring local reasoning behaviors, such as CoT generation, verification, planning, or critique, while differing in call structure, role composition, and num-ber of turns. A model trained on only one workflow can over-specialize to that controller and fail to transfer when familiar reasoning behaviors appear in novel compositions. This raises a natural question: how should the base model itself be post-trained to cooperate effectively with a family

We call these shared com-ponents modules . A controller is therefore a composition

Our controllers are implemented as executable Python programs, following the code-as-controller paradigm of ADAS (Hu et al., 2025) where each controller is a function that sequences module calls, routes outputs between turns, and handles branching or iteration logic, making the full controller inventory trans-parent, reproducible, and automatically extensible to new compositions. Since controllers share reusable modules, but differ in how those modules are arranged, CALM opens a space of module-level training strategies that does not exist in single-controller post-training. We study four variants that decompose the multi-controller loss by module type un-der a turn-level GRPO objective (Shao et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025): flat mixing of all turns, uniform weighting across module types, adaptive gradient-norm balancing, and single-role-per-step training. Because controllers differ in turn count and role composition, some modules naturally produce larger gradient updates than others; the variants explore whether explicitly accounting for this imbalance im-proves cross-controller generalization. We aim to map the design space that the compositional controller view opens and to identify which axes matter most for cross-controller transfer. Our experiments are designed to measure whether CALM truly learns reusable controller-aware reasoning rather than simply memorizing training workflows. We evaluate both on held-out controller compositions built from familiar reason-ing primitives and on more challenging controller shifts that alter the inventory of local roles or interfaces. The former isolates whether CALM can recombine known reasoning behaviors in novel workflows, while the latter measures whether the shared policy remains robust under broader structural shift. We further evaluate across mathematical reasoning tasks to assess whether the findings generalize beyond a single task distribution. ## 3. Problem Formulation 

We formalize CALM as a multi-task reinforcement learn-ing problem over a family of controller-induced interaction protocols. 

Controllers and modules. A controller c is a structured inference-time procedure that orchestrates one or more LLM calls to solve a given problem. Each controller defines a set of modules Mc = {m1, . . . , m K } and a control flow fc. A module mk = ( ρk, κ k) pairs a role ρk (e.g., 

Chain-of-Thought Agent , Critic Agent ) with a turn interface κk that specifies a role-specific instruction prompt and a structured output schema (e.g., [thinking, answer] , [feedback, correct] , etc.). The control flow fc is a program that determines the invocation order of modules, routes the output of one module as input to the next, and may include, but not limited to, non-LLM logic such as conditional branching or iteration, tool calls, mathe-matical operators, etc. For example, Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) defines three modules (a CoT Agent , a 

Critic Agent , and the same CoT Agent re-invoked with a reflection prompt) and a control flow that loops the critique–revise cycle up to N times or until the critic signals correctness.

Training instability in multi-turn RL. Multi-turn GRPO with terminal rewards presents known training stability challenges. As the number of turns grows, the credit as-signment problem intensifies: a single binary reward must be distributed across turns that may contribute very differ-ently to the outcome. In practice, we observe that training can become unstable after a moderate number of optimiza-tion steps, consistent with findings in prior multi-turn RL work (Wan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Both single-controller and multi-controller configurations are affected. We do not claim to solve this instability; rather, we design our training protocol and evaluation to account for it (Sec-tion 6). 

4.2. Multi-controller training variants 

We investigate four experimental variants of multi-controller training that differ in how gradient updates are distributed across module types. We start with the simplest starting point which trains all the modules together as they appear in a batch without any explicit decomposition. However, the compositional structure of controllers and the fact that different controllers share reusable module types enables a natural decomposition of the multi-controller loss by mod-ule. We describe the four training variants below with the experimental section showing systematic comparisons of these four training strategies. 

Multi-controller mixing ( CALM -MIXED ). The first vari-ant trains on multi-controller data without module decom-position. All turns from all controllers are placed in a single flat batch and optimized with the undifferentiated turn-level GRPO loss averaged across sampled controllers. The effec-tive weight of each module is proportional to the number of turns it contributes, so frequent modules like COT natu-rally receive more gradient signal. ## 5. Related Work 

Inference-time computation and controllers. A grow-ing body of work augments LLM reasoning by structur-ing inference across multiple calls or intermediate steps. Foundational methods include Chain-of-Thought prompt-ing (Wei et al., 2022), Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023), and Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023). More structured pipelines incorporate self-refinement (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2023), and tool-augmented reasoning (Schick et al., 2023). Recent work has begun automating the design and search of such inference-time procedures (Saad-Falcon et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025). CALM is complementary to this line of work: rather than optimizing the controller given a fixed model, we optimize the model given a distribution of controllers. 

Single-controller fine-tuning. Prior post-training work largely assumes a single fixed interaction pattern. Chow et al. (2025) study fine-tuning strategies specific to Best-of-N sampling; Shinn et al. (2023) train agents for Reflexion-style verbal reinforcement; and RLHF and related meth-ods (Brown et al., 2020) target single-turn or single-role interaction. These approaches produce models that may over-specialize to one workflow and fail to transfer when familiar reasoning behaviors appear in a novel controller composition. CALM addresses this directly by placing the training objective over a family of controllers rather than a single fixed one. 5Training Language Models to Cooperate with Inference-Time Controllers 

Multi-task and robust reinforcement learning. Multi-task RL trains a shared policy over a distribution of tasks or MDPs (Caruana, 1997; Teh et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2020). Ro-bust policy optimization seeks policies that remain effective under distributional shift (Tessler et al., 2019; Ghosh et al., 2021). CALM is related to both: we train a shared policy over controller-induced MDPs and evaluate on held-out con-troller distributions. However, the multi-controller setting introduces structure not present in standard multi-task RL. Controllers are not independent tasks — they share reusable local reasoning modules while differing in composition and turn structure. This heterogeneity produces module-level gradient imbalance that standard multi-task objectives do not address. Our compositional multi-module RL objective is specifically designed for this setting, and to the best of our knowledge, no prior work has studied post-training over a distribution of inference-time controllers. 

Modular and compositional reinforcement learning. Aseparate line of work studies policies composed from reusable sub-policies or skill primitives (Devin et al., 2017; Andreas et al., 2017; Eysenbach et al., 2019). These ap-proaches often require explicit task decomposition, modu-lar architectures, or learned routing between sub-policies. CALM differs in that we use a single shared policy condi-tioned on local turn interfaces, without requiring architec-tural modularity or explicit module routing. The composi-tional structure is instead reflected in the training objective through module-masked gradient updates and gradient-norm balancing. 

Meta-learning for language models. Meta-learning ap-proaches such as MAML (Finn et al., 2017) and its variants optimize for fast adaptation to new tasks from limited expe-rience. While superficially related, the goal of meta-learning is distinct from CALM’s objective: meta-learning targets rapid fine-tuning at test time, whereas CALM trains a sin-gle fixed policy that transfers to new controller composi-tions without any test-time gradient update. This distinction matters in practice, since most deployment settings do not permit test-time optimization of model parameters. 

Multi-turn and multi-agent RL for LLMs. Recent work has explored RL objectives for multi-turn LLM interac-tion (Wan et al., 2025) and multi-agent settings (Wang et al., 2025). CALM builds on the turn-level GRPO formula-tion of Wan et al. (2025), which aggregates token-level importance ratios at the turn level to handle variable-length multi-turn trajectories. We extend this formulation to the multi-controller setting by introducing module-level loss de-composition and gradient-norm balancing to handle the het-erogeneous turn structure induced by different controllers. 

## 6. Experiments 

We conduct two experiments to evaluate controller-aware post-training. Experiment 1 compares all four CALM vari-ants against single-controller baselines across twelve eval-uation controllers, evaluating in-distribution performance of multi-controller training. Experiment 2 tests whether such training generalize to unseen controllers and out-of-distribution data. 

6.1. Experimental Setup Datasets and models. For both experiments, we train on 2,048 problems from the GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) training split using L LAMA -3.2-3B-I NSTRUCT (Grattafiori et al., 2024). In Experiment 1, we evaluate the trained models on the GSM8K test set. For Experiment 2, we use MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2023) and AMC23 1 for testing on out-of-distribution data. Reward is binary in both experi-ments: R = 1 if the final extracted answer is correct, R = 0 

otherwise. In addition to the binary correctness reward, we add a lightweight format reward at every turn to encourage well-formed structured outputs. The format reward penal-izes JSON parse errors (−0.5) , missing required output fields (−0.2) , and extraneous fields (−0.1) , and assigns zero otherwise. This ensures the shared policy maintains valid turn interfaces across all module types throughout training, without conflating formatting compliance with task correctness.

## 7. Conclusion 

We present CALM as a framework to address a fundamental mismatch in how LLMs are post-trained. As the discovery of inference-time techniques continue to advance, the ques-tion of how a base model should be trained to cooperate effectively with a diverse and evolving family of controllers remains largely open. We present four different variations of multi-controller post training. Our experiments on GSM8K show that multi-controller training consistently outperforms single-controller baselines, which degrade sharply when evaluated outside their native workflow, and that the bene-fit extends to held-out controller compositions and broader controller shifts involving new module types. No single module-weighting variant dominates across all settings, sug-gesting that the choice of training strategy should be in-formed by the anticipated test-time controller distribution. More broadly, CALM points to a productive direction for future work: bridging the gap between two complementary lines of research: the automatic discovery of inference-time techniques, and the post-training of base models to use them effectively. 

## 8. Impact Statement 

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Compositional Learning for LLM Agents. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.
